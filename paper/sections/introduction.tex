%=============================================================================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%=============================================================================

Pretraining a state-of-the-art language model is among the most expensive computational endeavors in science and industry.
A single frontier run consumes \$100--500M in compute, occupies thousands of GPUs for months, and processes trillions of tokens~\citep{deepseekv3, llama4, olmo2}.
Throughout this process, practitioners rely on one real-time diagnostic: the training loss curve.
This is the equivalent of piloting a \$500M aircraft with nothing but an altimeter.

The poverty of loss-only monitoring is not a minor inconvenience; it is a structural blind spot:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item Loss can \emph{decrease} while model structure \emph{degrades}---memorization reduces loss without improving generalization~\citep{overtraining_fragile2025}.
    \item Loss is \emph{scale-dependent}: a loss of 2.0 means different things for 1B vs.\ 7B models, making cross-model comparison impossible.
    \item The decision of \emph{when to decay the learning rate} is made by fixed schedules, not by any signal from the model itself.
    \item There is no \emph{early warning} for training instability---loss spikes are detected only after the damage is done.
\end{itemize}

Meanwhile, a convergent body of results from Random Matrix Theory~\citep{martin_mahoney_jmlr2021} and statistical physics~\citep{sadrtdinov2025, liu_tegmark2025} has established that neural network weight matrices develop distinctive spectral signatures during training.
Specifically, well-trained layers exhibit \emph{heavy-tailed} eigenvalue distributions characterized by a power-law exponent $\alpha \in [2, 4]$, while untrained or poorly-trained layers remain close to the random Marchenko-Pastur distribution ($\alpha > 6$)~\citep{martin_mahoney_jmlr2021}.
The tool WeightWatcher~\citep{weightwatcher} demonstrated that $\alpha$ correlates with model quality across architectures, but only for final models---the \emph{dynamics} of $\alpha$ during training, and its potential as a \emph{real-time monitoring signal}, have not been explored.

\textbf{This paper fills that gap.}
We measure spectral properties of weight matrices across 9 language models spanning three architectures and two orders of magnitude in scale, tracking their evolution throughout training.
We discover:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{A universal compression constant} (\S\ref{sec:universal}). The stable rank normalized by hidden dimension (SR/$d$) converges to $\approx 0.056$ for all tested models, regardless of scale (70M--7B) or architecture (GPT-NeoX, LLaMA, OLMo2). This means training universally compresses weight matrices to ${\sim}5\%$ of their available dimensionality.

    \item \textbf{SR/$d$ as a universal quality predictor} (\S\ref{sec:predictor}). SR/$d$ achieves Spearman $r = -0.92$ with downstream benchmark performance across 143 checkpoints from 6 model scales---predicting quality from weights alone, without training data, test data, or loss values.

    \item \textbf{$\alpha$ reversal as early warning} (\S\ref{sec:reversal}). When the power-law exponent $\alpha$ begins \emph{increasing} ($d\alpha/dt > 0$), the model's spectral structure is degrading. This signal, invisible to loss, occurs in all models with fewer than ${\sim}250$ tokens/parameter. It diagnoses capacity exhaustion and signals the optimal point to begin LR decay.

    \item \textbf{An $\alpha$-guided adaptive schedule} (\S\ref{sec:schedule}). Maintaining peak LR until the $\alpha$ reversal signal, then decaying, outperforms cosine decay in both final loss ($-0.008$) and spectral structure ($\alpha$: 2.35 vs.\ 2.94). This demonstrates that spectral metrics can \emph{guide} training decisions, not merely observe them.
\end{enumerate}

Additionally, we derive a \textbf{``Structural Chinchilla'' scaling law} (\S\ref{sec:scaling}): $\alpha = 2.54 + 3.5\exp(-D/269N)$, where $D$ is training tokens and $N$ is parameters. This law identifies a \emph{structural phase transition} at $D/N \approx 250$--$500$ tokens/parameter, far beyond compute-optimal Chinchilla ratios (20 tokens/param). It explains quantitatively why ``over-training'' strategies like Llama-3 (15,000 tokens/param) produce models with superior generalization: they achieve heavy-tailed self-regularization that Chinchilla-optimal models never reach.

Together, these results establish spectral monitoring as a practical complement to loss curves.
The question that should guide every training decision shifts from ``has loss converged?'' to ``is spectral structure still forming, and is the model structurally mature?''
We release measurement code and monitoring dashboards for integration into existing training pipelines.
