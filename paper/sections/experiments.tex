\paragraph{Regression formula.}
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{score} \approx -0.258 \cdot \log_{10}(\mathrm{SR}/d) + 0.048 \cdot \log_{10}(N) - 0.252
    \label{eq:regression}
\end{equation}
This enables real-time performance prediction during training without running expensive evaluation suites.

\subsection{P3: $\alpha$ Reversal---Detecting Structural Degradation}
\label{sec:reversal}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Observation.} For models with insufficient tokens per parameter, the power-law exponent $\alpha$ reaches a minimum early in training, then \emph{increases}---signaling that spectral structure is \emph{degrading} even as loss continues to decrease. Critically, both OLMo-2-32B and 13B reveal that the reversal threshold is \emph{model-size-dependent}: OLMo-2-32B ($D/N = 189$) briefly \emph{enters} the heavy-tail regime ($\alpha_{\min} = 2.90 < 3$) then reverses entirely ($\Delta\alpha = +2.35$), while OLMo-2-13B ($D/N = 365$) shows the largest reversal magnitude ($\Delta\alpha = +2.71$) despite having twice the tokens per parameter of 32B.

\paragraph{Mechanism: MLP layers drive the reversal.}
Layer-type analysis reveals that $\alpha_{\mathrm{attn}}$ stabilizes early and remains flat, while $\alpha_{\mathrm{mlp}}$ continues rising after the global minimum (Figure~\ref{fig:mlp_attn_gap}). MLP layers store factual knowledge and have higher capacity demands; with insufficient training data, gradient noise destabilizes partially-learned MLP structure while attention patterns remain robust. The MLP/Attention gap ($\alpha_{\mathrm{mlp}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{attn}}$) reaches 0.7 at 2.8B, 1.7 at 13B, and 4.2 at 32B---confirming that MLP is the structural bottleneck at scale. At 32B, attention layers have already entered the heavy-tail regime ($\alpha_{\mathrm{attn}} = 3.4 < 4$) while MLP layers remain structurally random ($\alpha_{\mathrm{mlp}} = 7.6 > 6$).

\paragraph{Practical value.} The $\alpha$ reversal signal is \emph{invisible to loss} (loss continues decreasing via memorization) but reveals that continued training at the current LR is structurally counter-productive. Detecting reversal enables:
(a)~adaptive LR decay (start cooling when structure saturates),
(b)~early stopping of wasteful training runs,
(c)~diagnosing under-capacity (model too small for the data diversity).

\subsection{Structural Chinchilla Law}
\label{sec:scaling}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Fitting $\alpha_{\mathrm{final}}$ vs.\ $D/N$ (tokens per parameter) across models $\leq$1B yields a small-model approximation:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha(D/N) \approx 2.54 + 3.5 \cdot \exp\!\left(-\frac{D}{269 \cdot N}\right), \quad R^2 = 0.81 \;\text{(models $\leq$1B)}
    \label{eq:structural_chinchilla}
\end{equation}
However, extending to models above 1B reveals that $\alpha$ depends on \emph{both} $D/N$ and model size $N$ independently (Figure~\ref{fig:structural_chinchilla}). OLMo-2-13B at $D/N = 365$ achieves $\alpha = 6.95$, while Pythia-1B at similar $D/N = 297$ achieves $\alpha = 2.78$---the same training ratio produces vastly different structural states depending on model size.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig6_structural_chinchilla.pdf}
    \caption{Structural Chinchilla: $\alpha_{\mathrm{final}}$ vs.\ $D/N$. The exponential formula (blue curve) fits small models ($N \leq 1$B) but fails for larger models, which cluster at $\alpha > 5$ regardless of $D/N$. A sharp phase transition separates the two regimes at $N \approx 1.7$B.}
    \label{fig:structural_chinchilla}
\end{figure}

This ``Structural Chinchilla'' law quantifies the tokens-per-parameter budget needed for spectral maturity:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0pt]
    \item $D/N = 20$ (Chinchilla-optimal): $\alpha \approx 5.8$ --- \emph{structurally immature}
    \item $D/N = 269$ ($\tau$, characteristic scale): $\alpha \approx 3.8$ --- halfway to optimal
    \item $D/N = 500$: $\alpha \approx 3.1$ --- structurally mature
    \item $D/N \to \infty$: $\alpha \to 2.54$ --- asymptotic limit
\end{itemize}

The characteristic scale $\tau = 269$ tokens/parameter represents the budget at which heavy-tailed self-regularization is half-complete. Structure-optimal training requires ${\sim}12$--$25\times$ more tokens than compute-optimal (Chinchilla), explaining the empirical success of massive over-training strategies (Llama-3: 15,000 $D/N$; Pythia-70M: 4,261 $D/N$).

\paragraph{Phase transition at $N \approx 1.7$B.} Extending to 13B--65B models reveals that $\alpha_{\mathrm{final}}$ depends on \emph{both} $D/N$ and model size $N$ independently. OLMo-2-13B at $D/N = 365$ achieves $\alpha = 6.95$, while Pythia-1B at $D/N = 297$ achieves $\alpha = 2.78$---the same training ratio produces vastly different structural states. Fitting all 13 models with a sigmoid model yields:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha(N) \approx 2.65 + \Delta\alpha \cdot \sigma\!\left(\frac{\log_{10} N - 9.23}{0.07}\right), \quad R^2 = 0.97
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function and $\Delta\alpha \approx 2.1$. This reveals a \emph{sharp phase transition} at $N \approx 1.7$B parameters: models below this scale easily achieve heavy-tail structure ($\alpha < 3$) regardless of training budget, while models above it exhibit persistent structural immaturity ($\alpha > 5$) even with hundreds of tokens per parameter. The original exponential formula (Eq.~\ref{eq:structural_chinchilla}) remains valid as a small-model approximation ($R^2 = 0.81$ for $N \leq 1$B) but fails globally ($R^2 = 0.26$ on all 10 models with measured $\alpha$).

\subsection{Three-Phase Training Dynamics}
\label{sec:phases}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Measurements from Amber-7B (25 checkpoints spanning 1.26T tokens) and OLMo-2-13B (25 checkpoints spanning 5T tokens) reveal three spectral phases:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
    \item \textbf{Explosive ordering} (0--5\% of training, ${\sim}6$ tokens/param): $\alpha$ drops from ${\sim}18$ to its minimum at rate $\Delta\alpha/\Delta t \approx -2.3$/checkpoint. Both attention and MLP participate. SR drops 60\%+. For OLMo-2-13B, this phase spans steps 0--7K, with $\alpha$ dropping from 18.8 to 4.25.

    \item \textbf{Slow reversal} (5--80\%+ of training): $\alpha$ \emph{rises} from its minimum at rate $\Delta\alpha/\Delta t \approx +0.002$/checkpoint. MLP drives the reversal; attention remains stable. For OLMo-2-13B, the reversal spans steps 7K--596K (the \emph{entire} remaining training), with $\alpha$ rising from 4.25 to 6.95---the largest reversal magnitude observed ($\Delta\alpha = +2.71$).

    \item \textbf{Recovery} (only if $D/N$ is sufficient): $\alpha$ begins decreasing again. The accumulated gradient signal finally overcomes noise-driven degradation. OLMo-2-13B \emph{never reaches this phase}: even at $D/N = 365$ tokens/parameter, the model remains in Phase~2 throughout, confirming that larger models require substantially more data to achieve structural recovery.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Per-layer heatmap confirmation.} We validate the three-phase dynamics through per-layer spectral measurements across 24 checkpoints for Pythia-1B (66 layers) and Pythia-6.9B (130 layers). The heatmaps (Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}) reveal a striking pattern: \textbf{96\% of structural ordering occurs in the first 4\% of training} (step 0$\to$6K out of 143K total). At initialization, both models show high $\alpha$ across all layers ($\alpha_{\mathrm{mean}} = 3.97$ for 1B, $19.0$ for 6.9B). By step 6K (4\% of training), per-layer $\alpha$ values have already collapsed to near-final values---the heatmap is essentially ``frozen'' for the remaining 96\% of training. For Pythia-1B, the attn/MLP decomposition shows attention layers rapidly reaching $\alpha_{\mathrm{attn}} = 2.09$ by step 6K (remaining at $2.08$ at step 138K), while MLP layers reach $\alpha_{\mathrm{mlp}} = 3.00$ then slowly drift to $3.34$---a layer-resolved view of the MLP-driven reversal mechanism.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_v2/fig13_heatmaps_v2.pdf}
    \caption{Per-layer $\alpha$ heatmaps for Pythia-1B (top) and Pythia-6.9B (bottom) across 24 training checkpoints. Virtually all structural ordering occurs in the explosive phase (step 0$\to$6K); the remaining 132K steps produce minimal spectral change. Attention layers (blue arrows) consistently achieve lower $\alpha$ than MLP layers (red arrows).}

\end{figure}

The ``7\% rule'': at the $\alpha$ minimum (typically 4--14\% of total training), models already achieve 83--100\% of their final downstream performance. The remaining 86--96\% of compute produces diminishing structural returns.

\subsection{SR/$d$ as Post-Hoc Training Audit}
\label{sec:audit}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

SR/$d$ can diagnose training sufficiency from weights alone, without access to loss curves or benchmarks. We validate this by applying it post-hoc to K2-65B~\citep{k2_report}, a 65B-parameter model trained on 1.4T tokens ($D/N = 21$, Chinchilla-optimal).

\paragraph{Diagnosis.} K2-65B achieves SR/$d = 0.036$---below the converged values of all other models at comparable $d$. Its $\alpha$ trajectory shows reversal ($\alpha_{\min} = 4.45 \to \alpha_{\mathrm{final}} = 5.09$, $\Delta\alpha = +0.65$), and SR/$d$ is still rising at training end (0.030 $\to$ 0.036), indicating compression has not converged. Our spectral diagnosis: \emph{structurally immature, training insufficient}.

\paragraph{External validation.} This diagnosis is independently corroborated by: (i)~K2 underperforms Llama-2-70B (which uses only 43\% more tokens) on 15/21 benchmarks; (ii)~training curves in the K2 technical report show non-plateaued metrics at the final checkpoint; (iii)~the authors acknowledge a limited data budget. The spectral audit reaches the same conclusion as comprehensive benchmarking---but requires only the model weights, no evaluation infrastructure.
